Sharing a warehouse with robots

How do you decide whether to wait, pass or change your route when you do not know what an automated vehicle will do next?

As part of my doctoral research in the Mixed Fleet project, I developed a Unity warehouse simulation to investigate how people understand and respond to automated guided vehicles (AGVs). A study with 20 vocational logistics students explored how their expectations, trust and behaviour changed through these encounters.

The task

In mixed-fleet warehouses, people operate vehicles alongside automation. Sharing that space requires them to interpret vehicle movements, understand signals and decide when to proceed.

Studying these encounters in a working warehouse is challenging. Research needs to fit around daily operations, access to machinery and safety requirements. Recreating the same potentially confusing situation for every participant can also be difficult.

A simulation allowed us to create repeatable encounters without placing participants alongside moving industrial vehicles. We could observe their decisions, ask them to explain their thinking and compare their experiences across the same scenarios.

The question went beyond whether people successfully navigated the warehouse. A driver who gives way might understand the AGV’s intention, or might stop precisely because its intention is unclear.

Developing the simulation

An industry workshop with 17 specialists informed three scenarios:

  • Narrow aisle: encountering an AGV where space for passing is limited.
  • Intersection: approaching a crossing where the driver and AGV need to coordinate.
  • Blocked route: encountering an interruption and deciding how to proceed.

Participants navigated these situations from the perspective of a reach-truck driver.

I built on existing Unity assets and libraries, developing the controls and interactions needed for the study. My work included vehicle controls, interaction logic, AGV light signals and the AGV’s visual design. I drew on how real AGVs and warehouse machinery operate when implementing vehicle movement, stopping and responses within the scenarios.

The simulation was designed to make these encounters understandable and repeatable. It represents selected aspects of warehouse work, with room to extend the vehicle behaviour, environmental detail and range of situations.

The user study

We conducted a study with 20 vocational logistics students, combining:

  • Observation and think-aloud accounts to examine participants’ actions and their reasoning during the encounters.
  • Follow-up interviews to discuss confusing moments, expectations and experiences.
  • Pre- and post-study questionnaires to examine changes in trust and technology acceptance.

Using these methods together helped us interpret behaviour more carefully. Observing someone wait told us what they did; listening to their explanation helped us understand why.

What we found

Coordination did not always mean understanding

Participants slowed down, yielded, changed their routes and attempted to pass the AGV. These adjustments often allowed them to continue, even when they remained uncertain about its signals or intentions.

This distinction matters: apparently smooth coordination can conceal confusion. Successful navigation alone does not establish that a vehicle’s communication is understandable.

Confidence depended on how the vehicle behaved

Appropriate stopping could strengthen confidence, while unclear behaviour prompted caution. Some participants made their trust conditional on predictable movement, clear communication or access to safety controls.

Their accounts pointed to a concrete design priority: help people understand what the AGV will do next, so they can decide how to respond.

Experience affected participants differently

Overall trust and technology acceptance did not change significantly. However, comparisons between Generation Z participants and Millennials showed different trajectories: scores increased among the younger group and decreased among the older group.

These findings remain exploratory because the groups were small and unequal: 14 Generation Z participants and six Millennials.

The outcome

The project produced a reusable simulation for studying how people interpret and navigate encounters with automated vehicles. It also showed the value of examining actions alongside people’s explanations: participants could adapt their behaviour while still being unsure how the automation worked.

The findings inform the design of AGV communication and introductory training, particularly around vehicle intentions, signals and responses to blocked movement. They do not establish that a particular communication design solves these problems; that requires further evaluation.

Future development could add more varied traffic and vehicle behaviour, while repeated sessions could explore how understanding changes with experience. Research in operating warehouses would help assess how well these findings transfer to everyday work.
