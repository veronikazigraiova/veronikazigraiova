Making robot activity easier to understand

When someone is busy driving, information about nearby automation needs to be easy to notice and interpret.

This project explored haptic feedback, scent cues and an interactive map as ways to support awareness in a simulated mixed-fleet warehouse. It built on our earlier simulation study, where participants could navigate around automated guided vehicles (AGVs) while remaining uncertain about their signals and intentions.

The task

The earlier study highlighted a need for clearer information about what AGVs were doing. Participants’ experiences and suggestions informed the next iteration of the simulation: additional feedback to help drivers locate nearby vehicles and understand situations such as an AGV stopping because of an error.

We explored two related questions: could people notice nearby AGVs without continually looking for them, and could they access more detailed information when they needed it?

The design combined directional feedback through haptic sleeves with an interactive map. We also explored scent as a possible way to communicate AGV presence without adding another visual signal.

Scent feedback was explored in an initial pilot. After that pilot, the main study involved 20 participants and focused on haptic feedback and the interactive map.

Exploring scent as a signal

We used an Aroma Shooter to introduce scent cues representing AGV presence in the simulated environment. This was an exploratory idea: could smell provide a useful background cue and potentially reduce the effort needed to monitor nearby automation?

In this setup, the cues did not work as we had hoped. Participants either did not notice the scent or paid little attention to it. They did not reliably connect it with the presence of an AGV.

Delivering a cue did not mean it became meaningful information. The scent needed both to attract attention and to be understood as a signal about the vehicle; that connection was missing in participants’ accounts.

We therefore did not carry scent feedback forward into the main study. This finding does not rule out olfactory feedback more broadly. Different scents, delivery timing or clearer familiarisation could be explored, but our study does not establish whether those changes would make it useful.

Refining the design

We continued with haptic sleeves and the interactive map, which participants found useful for different purposes.

Haptic sleeves: sensing where an AGV is approaching from

The sleeves provided directional vibration cues to help participants recognise where an AGV was coming from.

Participants responded positively to this feedback. They described it as making the spatial situation easier to understand and reducing the mental effort involved in keeping track of nearby automation.

These accounts suggest that directional haptic feedback could support awareness while drivers attend to their own task. They reflect participants’ perceived experience; they do not, by themselves, demonstrate an objectively measured reduction in cognitive load.

Interactive map: checking locations and understanding interruptions

The map allowed participants to check AGV locations and access additional information when needed. For example, it could explain that a stationary AGV was stuck or experiencing an error.

This addressed a gap identified in the earlier study: seeing that a vehicle had stopped did not necessarily help participants understand why.

Participants found the map useful as a reference they could consult. While the sleeves drew attention to the direction of an approaching AGV, the map supported a more deliberate check of vehicle locations and status.

The outcome

The project used an iterative design process, with findings and participant suggestions from the earlier simulation study informing the new feedback.

The exploration also helped narrow the design. Scent cues did not reliably communicate AGV presence in this setup, so we focused the main study on haptic feedback and the interactive map.

Participants’ responses supported giving these two forms of feedback distinct roles: directional cues for immediate awareness, and information on demand for understanding the wider situation. Further evaluation could examine when drivers rely on each, whether the cues remain useful over repeated sessions and how they affect performance alongside perceived workload.
