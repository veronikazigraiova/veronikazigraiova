Co-designing language learning with AI and social robots

My MSc research explored how AI could support language learning while preserving the role of teachers and human interaction.

I conducted a co-design workshop with six participants: two Finnish language teachers, three students and an industry specialist. Through demonstrations involving NAO and the Elias language-learning tools, interviews and collaborative design activities, I investigated what teachers and students wanted from AI-supported education.

I then developed a language-assistant concept and evaluated it with 12 Finnish language students, comparing learning with flashcards alone against flashcards supported by an AI assistant.

The task

Language students need opportunities to practise, ask questions and receive feedback. Teachers need to support students with different abilities and learning needs while preparing lessons and assessing progress.

The project asked where AI could help with these activities, what teachers and students would accept, and which responsibilities should remain with people.

The design challenge was to identify useful support without weakening the human relationships involved in learning. A tool might provide a quick explanation, for example, but students still need to judge whether that explanation is accurate.

My role

I carried out the project as my MSc thesis in Human–Technology Interaction at Tampere University, working with Robostudio and Utelias Technologies.

My work covered planning and conducting the co-design workshop, preparing demonstrations and collaborative activities, analysing user needs, developing the concept and evaluating it with students. I analysed qualitative data through thematic analysis and affinity diagrams, and compared the evaluation results statistically in SPSS.

The approach

The research followed a human-centred design process:

  1. Understand needs and concerns through interviews, demonstrations and co-design with teachers, students and an industry specialist.
  2. Translate the findings into a concept for an AI-supported language classroom.
  3. Prototype the language-assistant interaction using a purpose-written ChatGPT prompt.
  4. Evaluate the assistant through learning activities, questionnaires, a vocabulary test and interviews.

Co-design: exploring what support should look like

The two-and-a-half-hour hybrid workshop took place at Robostudio, with participants collaborating through a Mural canvas.

Participants tried a Finnish vocabulary activity using NAO and the Elias Robot application, and explored an AI language-assessment tool. These demonstrations gave them concrete experiences to discuss before designing possible future uses.

The collaborative activities focused on:

  • Personas: identifying the needs of language teachers and students.
  • User journeys: mapping interactions and finding points where support was needed.
  • Concept design: proposing how AI features could fit into language learning.

What teachers and students wanted

Participants saw opportunities for immediate feedback, more flexible practice and support with repetitive tasks. They wanted learning activities tailored to students’ proficiency and difficulties, with explanations that went beyond simply assigning a language level.

They also raised concerns about inaccurate answers, data privacy and overreliance on AI. Teachers questioned how they would handle situations where an AI explanation contradicted their own.

Human involvement remained a priority, particularly for sensitive conversations, authentic spoken interaction and assessment that accounts for differences in pronunciation and communication.

From findings to a design concept

I developed a concept for an AI-supported classroom and proposed changes to the Elias learning platform. The concept included personalised exercises, feedback and progress information, a collaborative learning space and an assistant available during independent study.

The assistant addressed a specific need from the workshop: being able to ask a question when it arises and receive an explanation immediately.

To explore this interaction, I created a prompt that configured ChatGPT as a Finnish language assistant. This provided a way to test one part of the concept before developing a complete platform. The broader classroom features remained design proposals.

Evaluation: testing the language assistant

I conducted two online learning sessions with 12 beginner-level Finnish students, randomly divided into two groups of six.

Both groups received the same flashcards and 30 minutes to study. One group used only the flashcards; the other also had access to the prompted AI assistant. Participants then completed the same Finnish language test.

Questionnaires and interviews explored how students experienced the assistant, including its usefulness, their confidence and any difficulties they encountered.

Higher scores in the AI-supported group

The group with access to the assistant achieved a mean test score of 7.67, compared with 4.50 in the flashcard-only group. The difference was statistically significant.

This was a small, short evaluation of immediate test performance. It did not establish whether the benefit would persist over time or transfer to everyday conversation.

Immediate feedback was the clearest benefit

Students valued being able to ask questions and receive explanations while studying, particularly when working through grammar difficulties.

All six students who used the assistant rated it as moderately or very useful, and five reported improved speaking confidence. These confidence ratings describe their perceptions rather than a measured change in speaking ability.

Useful support still had limitations

Some students encountered inconsistent grammar explanations or found that the assistant lost track of earlier conversation. Others felt that the interaction lacked the personal connection they valued when learning with a teacher.

These findings supported keeping access to human guidance alongside AI-assisted practice.

The outcome

The thesis produced a classroom concept, an evaluated language-assistant prototype and 11 design implications drawn from the research and literature.

Selected implications include:

  • Explain AI’s capabilities and limitations so teachers and students can use it critically.
  • Support self-paced, personalised practice that fits students’ proficiency and schedules.
  • Provide immediate, informative feedback with explanations and suggestions for improvement.
  • Support teachers’ creativity when preparing activities and learning materials.
  • Preserve authentic human communication within language learning.
  • Make progress visible so students can understand what they have learned and what needs further practice.

The project connected users’ needs to a testable interaction concept. It also identified where further work was needed: reliable explanations, meaningful human involvement and evaluation over longer periods.
